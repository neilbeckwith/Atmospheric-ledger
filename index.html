CO₂ in the air, and the warming it drives

2024 was the record growth year: the global mean rose 3.44 ppm in 2024 (3.53 ppm at Mauna Loa), the largest annual rise ever measured, because the 2023–24 El Niño drought collapsed the tropical land sink while emissions stayed near record levels. Growth eased to 2.84 ppm in 2025 as the land sink recovered — the same pattern the Terrestrial Ledger shows from the sink side. The temperature stayed high anyway: 2025 was the second-warmest year in the GISTEMP record, after 2024.

The processes — the science behind the numbers

Every ppm in the chart got there through emissions, and whether it stays there is decided by the sinks. The forcing it exerts is what warms the planet. 1 ppm CO₂ = 2.124 GtC = 7.79 GtCO₂.

Why the growth rate keeps rising

CO₂ growth averaged 0.86 ppm a year in the 1960s, 1.64 in the 1980s, 2.40 in the 2010s and 2.58 in 2015–2024 — because emissions grew faster than the sinks could absorb them. The sinks take roughly half of each year's emissions, but their share falls in hot, dry years: in 2024 the airborne fraction hit 67%, the highest in the record, because the tropical land sink collapsed in the El Niño drought while fossil emissions stayed at 10.54 GtC. The growth rate is therefore not just an emissions story — it is an emissions story minus a sink story, and the sink story is getting worse as the planet warms.

The forcing ledger

Effective radiative forcing, 1750–2019, in watts per square metre (W/m²) — how much each agent has changed the energy balance at the top of the atmosphere. Positive warms, negative cools. The greenhouse gases together add +3.32 W/m²; aerosols mask about a third of it, leaving a net human forcing of +2.72 W/m². Source: IPCC AR6 WG1, Chapter 7 (Forster et al. 2021).

AgentEffective radiative forcing (W/m²)Effect

What a watt per square metre means: +2.72 W/m² spread over the whole Earth is about 1,400 terawatts of extra heating — roughly 70 times humanity's total energy use. Almost all of it ends up as heat stored in the ocean, which is why the warming lags the forcing and why it will continue for decades even if emissions stop tomorrow. A doubling of CO₂ alone gives +3.93 W/m² (IPCC AR6).

How to read the columns. Global CO₂ is the NOAA marine-surface mean — the best estimate of the whole atmosphere; Mauna Loa sits about 1.7 ppm above it because the Northern Hemisphere, where most emissions happen, runs richer in CO₂. Growth is the year-on-year change in the annual mean. The airborne fraction is the atmospheric growth (in GtC) divided by total human emissions (fossil + land-use change) that year — it swings from under 30% in strong sink years to 67% in 2024.

CO₂ is a stock, not a flow. Emissions are a flow — about 11.8 GtC a year — but what warms the planet is the stock: the 425.63 ppm already in the air. Cutting emissions to zero tomorrow would stop the stock rising, but it would not bring it down: CO₂ is removed by the sinks only slowly, over centuries. That is why the growth rate, not just the emissions rate, is the number to watch — and why 2024's record 3.44 ppm jump mattered more than any single year's emissions total.

The variables that watch it — GCOS atmospheric Essential Climate Variables

The atmosphere is not measured by one instrument. It is constrained by this set of 16 atmospheric Essential Climate Variables (ECVs) — the minimum set needed to assess the atmosphere's state and change, coordinated by the Global Climate Observing System (GCOS). The groups are GCOS's own: Surface, Upper-air, Atmospheric Composition. Note the live GCOS list puts Clouds under Atmospheric Composition and Lightning under Upper-air. Source: GCOS ECV list, gcos.wmo.int (accessed 2026-11-27).

VariableGCOS groupRole in the ledger

How to read the table. The surface variables are what we live in — temperature, rain, wind and pressure. The upper-air variables are the fingerprint: the troposphere warms while the stratosphere cools, which is the signature of greenhouse warming rather than solar warming. The composition variables are the forcing itself — the gases and particles that set the energy balance. Note: unlike the ocean and terrestrial lists, the atmospheric list has no pilot tier in the current GCOS listing — all 16 are established ECVs.

Which variables constrain the warming?

The warming is pinned down first by surface temperature (the anomaly series itself), carbon dioxide, methane and other greenhouse gases (the forcing), Earth radiation budget (the energy imbalance the forcing creates), upper-air temperature (the warming-cooling fingerprint), surface and upper-air water vapour (the feedback that roughly doubles the direct CO₂ warming), clouds and aerosols (the two largest uncertainties in how much warming a given forcing brings), and ozone and its precursors (the chemistry that links pollution to forcing). Each decade's detail panel lists the ECVs that watch that decade.

Methods, model assumptions & sources

What is counted

The headline CO₂ figures are the NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory annual means: the global marine-surface mean (425.63 ppm in 2025) and the Mauna Loa mean (427.35 ppm in 2025), with the year-on-year growth of each. The temperature figure is the NASA GISTEMP v4 annual (J-D) global mean surface temperature anomaly, land-ocean, relative to the 1951–1980 baseline (+1.19 °C in 2025). The airborne fraction is the Global Carbon Budget 2025 atmospheric growth divided by total human emissions (fossil + land-use change) for the same year. Methane is the NOAA GML global annual mean (1,935.95 ppb in 2025).

What is not in the headline figures

The Global Carbon Budget 2025 series ends in 2024, so the airborne fraction and the sink figures run through 2024 only; the 2025 CO₂ growth is the observed NOAA value, converted to carbon at 2.124 GtC per ppm for the cumulative total. The 2026 monthly Mauna Loa reading (October 2026, trend 429.98 ppm) is a single month's seasonally adjusted value, not an annual mean, and is shown as the latest reading only. The 2025 preliminary net land sink quoted in the Terrestrial Ledger is not used here.

Model & estimate assumptions

  • Live counter: the 2025 global growth rate (2.84 ppm/yr = 22.12 GtCO₂/yr) is spread evenly across the year — about 702 tonnes of CO₂ per second — and applied to 2026 until a newer annual total is published. It is an accounting spread, not a real-time measurement; real growth varies by season (the Northern Hemisphere growing season draws CO₂ down every summer) and by El Niño.
  • Cumulative live total: cumulative atmospheric growth at the end of 2025 (237.7 GtC: the GCB 1959–2024 sum of 231.7 GtC plus the observed 2025 growth of 6.03 GtC) plus the 2026 estimate from 1 January to now, at the same even rate.
  • Decade means: arithmetic means of the annual values in the stated years. The global-mean CO₂ growth is shown only where the global series exists (from 1979); the 1960s and 1970s show Mauna Loa growth only. The airborne fraction mean for "2020s so far" covers 2020–2024, the years the budget series covers.
  • Warming since 1880–1899: the latest annual anomaly minus the mean GISTEMP anomaly for 1880–1899 (−0.23 °C). This is a warming relative to the late 19th century, not to the 1850–1900 pre-industrial baseline used by the IPCC — the two differ by a small amount, and the page states which one it uses.
  • Forcing values: IPCC AR6 assessed effective radiative forcing, 1750–2019, from Chapter 7. They are assessed values with uncertainty ranges (for example CO₂ +2.16, range +1.90 to +2.41 W/m²); the page shows the central estimates.
  • Rounding: CO₂ shown to two decimals (ppm), growth to two decimals (ppm/yr), temperature to two decimals (°C), airborne fraction to one decimal (%); underlying values are in data.json.
